APRS over Mercury: the first test on the air

On 4 November 2026 two NeoAPRS stations 40.8 km apart exchanged APRS messages over the Mercury modem for about twenty minutes. Every message in the log was delivered and acknowledged. Ten watts into a small balcony antenna was sufficient, and a message sent at 1 W was decoded at a signal-to-noise ratio of −3 dB.

Background

The test began as a routine APRS message exchange between OH2GBA and OH2GAX on 2 m, relayed by the local digipeaters. When the VHF path at OH2GAX became unavailable, both stations switched to the Mercury radio in NeoAPRS on 50.620 MHz in the 6 m band and continued the same conversation without interruption. OH2GAX confirmed reception within the first minutes: Ack received fine here.

To our knowledge, this was the first exchange of APRS traffic over the Mercury modem between two stations on the air.

About NeoAPRS

NeoAPRS is a free and open-source APRS client: a map of the stations around you, messaging with acknowledgements, bulletins, objects and your own beacon. It runs on Windows, macOS, Linux and the Raspberry Pi, as a desktop app or in any browser, so the same station can be used from a phone or a laptop. It works with any KISS TNC on VHF and, since version 0.16, with the Mercury modem on an SSB radio.

About Mercury

Mercury is a free and open-source HF data modem developed by Rhizomatica. It uses the data modes of the FreeDV project: OFDM, several carriers sent side by side, with a strong LDPC error-correcting code. NeoAPRS builds Mercury in and hands it APRS frames, so an SSB radio with a sound card becomes an APRS station without a separate modem program.

Power reduction

To measure the link margin, both stations reduced their transmit power in steps while continuing to exchange messages.

OH2GBA100 W, then half power, 25, 10 and 5 W
OH2GAX45, 10, 5 and 1 W

At full power OH2GAX's signal was 6 to 7 dB above the noise. At 5 W, and at 1 W, it was 2 to 3 dB below the noise, and his messages were still decoded and acknowledged. This is the margin the Mercury modem is designed for.

Two of the messages from OH2GBA needed more than one attempt. One was acknowledged on the second attempt; the other, sent while the power was being reduced to 5 W, on the fourth.

Findings

  • Large link margin. Messages were decoded and acknowledged at 2 to 3 dB below the noise, far below what plain 300-baud packet needs.
  • Clean channel sharing. Every reply waited until the other station's frame had ended. No frame was lost to two stations transmitting at once.
  • One conversation, two radios. The exchange moved from 2 m to the Mercury radio and continued in the same conversation, in the same program.

For emergency communications

The test itself followed an emergency pattern: the usual path failed, and the traffic moved to another band without losing the conversation. That combination suits EMCOMM work well.

  • Everything APRS already does. Positions on a map, messages with acknowledgements, bulletins to everyone, and objects such as a shelter or a road block. Operators already know how to use it.
  • A modern modem underneath. Mercury decodes messages below the noise, at low power, on a simple antenna, where older HF packet fails.
  • No infrastructure needed. Mercury works station to station, with no digipeaters, no internet and no servers. A radio, a sound card and a computer are enough.
  • Two radios in one program. The local 2 m network and the longer path over Mercury share one map and one message list, and the operator picks the radio for each station.
  • Easy to deploy. NeoAPRS runs on Windows, macOS, Linux and the Raspberry Pi, and Mercury is built in: there is nothing else to install.

Next steps

NeoAPRS and its Mercury support are being actively developed, and every test on the air improves them. We are looking for more stations to try it on the air and tell us how it went: the band, the distance, the power and what worked or did not. Write to neoaprs@oh2gba.eu, open an issue on GitHub, or send your logs from NeoAPRS with Send logs in the Help group of the Settings tab.

Try it

Mercury is built into NeoAPRS: an SSB radio with a sound card is all it takes. The suggested frequencies are on the front page, and the Dummy HF radio lets you try it with no radio at all.
